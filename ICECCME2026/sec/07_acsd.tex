\section{Discussion on Adaptive Speculative Strategies Derived from Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:acsd}

The fixed-policy results preserve the key systems signal:
low-$k$ configurations dominate high-$k$ settings in end-to-end runtime,
while acceptance-related observables alone are insufficient to predict wall-clock
wins. We therefore keep ACSD as a control-policy extension intended for runtime
stability and policy robustness, not as a guaranteed peak-throughput replacement
for the best fixed setting.

\subsection{Design objective}

Let speculative-cycle latency be
\begin{equation}
  L = \frac{T_{\text{draft}} + T_{\text{verify}}}{\tau},
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the expected accepted tokens per cycle.
ACSD targets adverse samples where $\tau$ collapses for sustained windows,
by adapting policy online (draft length, rescue behavior, and fallback rules)
rather than fixing a single global $k$.

\subsection{Controller formalization}

We model ACSD as a finite-state controller with states
$\{\mathcal{D}_{pri},\mathcal{D}_{res},\mathcal{M}_{AR}\}$
(primary draft, rescue draft, autoregressive (AR) fallback), and transition rules
based on recent acceptance statistics.
Four mechanisms are retained:
(i) bounded adaptive $k_t$ updates,
(ii) rescue switching after repeated low-acceptance windows,
(iii) guarded fallback for persistent collapse,
and (iv) checkpointed execution for recoverability.

\subsection{Empirical interpretation}

Using the ablation tables, ACSD speedup depends on threshold policy.
In deterministic mode, representative settings span roughly
$S\approx2.15$--$2.36$; in stochastic mode,
$S\approx1.76$--$1.96$.
Compared against fixed-$k$ references, ACSD can be competitive in some
parameter regions but is not uniformly superior across all quality and
latency criteria.

Paired tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction show that, in the reported
comparison set, token-timing differences can be significant while aggregate
quality deltas are not consistently significant after correction.
This supports a conservative deployment claim: ACSD is a policy instrument for
stability trade-offs, not a universal speedup guarantee.

\subsection{How this guidance should be used}

For deployment, fixed deterministic $k{=}4$ remains the default first choice
when maximizing mean throughput is the sole objective on RTX4090-class runs.
ACSD is appropriate when operators prefer an adaptive policy that can reduce
risk from difficult inputs and parameter-sensitive runtime drift, accepting
that gains are scenario-dependent.

\subsection{Scope in this paper}

Quantitative ACSD claims in this paper are limited to the reported
Qwen3-family artifacts and ablation tables.
Broader claims across model families, longer contexts, and additional hardware
still require dedicated validation.
